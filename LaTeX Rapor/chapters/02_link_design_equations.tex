\section{Link Design Equations}

This chapter details the fundamental RF engineering equations and geometric transformations used to design the clear-sky static link budget. These models are derived based on the link design procedure outlined in \textit{Chapter 4} of the reference textbook by Pratt, Bostian, and Allnutt~\cite{pratt}.

\subsection{Slant-Path Geometry and Coordinate Transformations}
Calculating the pointing parameters (slant range, elevation angle, and azimuth angle) between the ground station and the geostationary satellite requires transformations between spherical coordinates and the Earth-Centered, Earth-Fixed (ECEF) Cartesian coordinate system. 

Let the Earth's equatorial radius be $R_e = \SI{6378.137}{\km}$, and the nominal radius of the geostationary orbit be $r_{sat} = \SI{42164.0}{\km}$. For a ground station located at latitude $\phi$, longitude $\lambda$, and altitude $h$ above mean sea level, the ECEF position vector $\vec{R}_{ground}$ is given by:
\begin{equation}
    \vec{R}_{ground} = \begin{bmatrix} (R_e + h)\cos\phi\cos\lambda \\ (R_e + h)\cos\phi\sin\lambda \\ (R_e + h)\sin\phi \end{bmatrix}
\end{equation}
The geostationary satellite, located in the equatorial plane ($\phi_{sat} = 0^\circ$) at longitude $\lambda_{sat}$, has the Cartesian position vector:
\begin{equation}
    \vec{R}_{sat} = \begin{bmatrix} r_{sat}\cos\lambda_{sat} \\ r_{sat}\sin\lambda_{sat} \\ 0 \end{bmatrix}
\end{equation}
The slant-range vector $\vec{\rho}$ pointing from the ground station to the satellite is computed as:
\begin{equation}
    \vec{\rho} = \vec{R}_{sat} - \vec{R}_{ground}
\end{equation}
The absolute physical distance (slant range) $d$ is the Euclidean norm of the slant-range vector:
\begin{equation}
    d = \|\vec{\rho}\| = \sqrt{\rho_x^2 + \rho_y^2 + \rho_z^2}
\end{equation}
To determine the pointing elevation angle $\theta$, we project the unit line-of-sight vector $\vec{a}_{\rho} = \vec{\rho}/d$ onto the local topocentric unit vector pointing vertically Up ($\vec{u}_{up}$). The unit Up vector at the ground station coordinates is:
\begin{equation}
    \vec{u}_{up} = \begin{bmatrix} \cos\phi\cos\lambda \\ \cos\phi\sin\lambda \\ \sin\phi \end{bmatrix}
\end{equation}
The local elevation angle $\theta$ is then calculated as:
\begin{equation}
    \theta = \arcsin\left( \vec{a}_{\rho} \cdot \vec{u}_{up} \right) = \arcsin\left( \frac{\vec{\rho} \cdot \vec{u}_{up}}{d} \right)
\end{equation}
The azimuth angle $Az$ is determined by projecting the line-of-sight vector onto the local East ($\vec{u}_{east}$) and North ($\vec{u}_{north}$) unit vectors:
\begin{align}
    \vec{u}_{east} &= \begin{bmatrix} -\sin\lambda \\ \cos\lambda \\ 0 \end{bmatrix} \\
    \vec{u}_{north} &= \begin{bmatrix} -\sin\phi\cos\lambda \\ -\sin\phi\sin\lambda \\ \cos\phi \end{bmatrix}
\end{align}
yielding the azimuth angle relative to true North:
\begin{equation}
    Az = \arctan_2\left( \vec{a}_{\rho} \cdot \vec{u}_{east},\ \vec{a}_{\rho} \cdot \vec{u}_{north} \right)
\end{equation}
The central angle $\psi$ between the ground station and the satellite ECEF position vectors is:
\begin{equation}
    \psi = \arccos\left( \frac{\vec{R}_{ground} \cdot \vec{R}_{sat}}{\|\vec{R}_{ground}\| \|\vec{R}_{sat}\|} \right)
\end{equation}

\subsection{Antenna Parameters}
For a circular parabolic dish antenna of diameter $D$ operating at carrier frequency $f$, the wavelength of the electromagnetic wave is:
\begin{equation}
    \lambda_w = \frac{c}{f}
\end{equation}
where $c \approx \SI{2.99792458e8}{\m/\s}$ is the speed of light in vacuum. The peak boresight gain $G$ in dBi is calculated as:
\begin{equation}
    G(dBi) = 10\log_{10}\left( \eta \left( \frac{\pi D}{\lambda_w} \right)^2 \right)
\end{equation}
where $\eta$ is the aperture efficiency (set to $0.62$, representing standard engineering efficiency). The half-power beamwidth ($\theta_{HPBW}$), which determines the angular separation at which the antenna gain drops by \SI{3}{\dB}, is approximated by:
\begin{equation}
    \theta_{HPBW} \approx 70^\circ \frac{\lambda_w}{D}
\end{equation}

\subsection{Free-Space Path Loss (FSPL)}
The Free-Space Path Loss (FSPL) represents the geometrical attenuation of signal power due to the spherical spreading of the wavefront over the distance $d$. In decibel terms, FSPL is expressed as:
\begin{equation}
    FSPL(dB) = 20\log_{10}\left( \frac{4\pi d}{\lambda_w} \right) = 92.45 + 20\log_{10}(f_{GHz}) + 20\log_{10}(d_{km})
\end{equation}
where $f_{GHz}$ is the frequency in GHz and $d_{km}$ is the slant range in km.

\subsection{RF Link Power Budget and Carrier-to-Noise Density}
For any link segment, the Equivalent Isotropically Radiated Power (EIRP) is the total effective power radiated in the direction of the main beam:
\begin{equation}
    EIRP(dBW) = P_{tx}(dBW) + G_{tx}(dBi) - L_{tx\_feeder}(dB)
\end{equation}
where $P_{tx}$ is the transmitter HPA output power in dBW, $G_{tx}$ is the transmit antenna gain, and $L_{tx\_feeder}$ represents physical attenuation in the waveguide feeder line.
The receiver figure of merit (quality factor) $G/T$ is:
\begin{equation}
    G/T(dB/K) = G_{rx}(dBi) - L_{rx\_feeder}(dB) - 10\log_{10}(T_{sys})
\end{equation}
where $G_{rx}$ is the receive antenna gain, $L_{rx\_feeder}$ is the receive feeder loss, and $T_{sys}$ is the system noise temperature in Kelvin.
The carrier-to-noise density ratio $C/N_0$ at the receiver demodulator input is:
\begin{equation}
    C/N_0(dB\text{-}Hz) = EIRP(dBW) + G/T(dB/K) - L_{total}(dB) - 10\log_{10}(k)
\end{equation}
where $L_{total}$ is the sum of path loss, pointing misalignments, polarization losses, and atmospheric clear-sky absorption. The term $-10\log_{10}(k) \approx \SI{228.6}{\dB(W/(Hz\cdot K))}$ represents Boltzmann's constant in decibels.
The carrier-to-noise ratio $C/N$ over the transponder bandwidth $B$ is:
\begin{equation}
    C/N(dB) = C/N_0(dB\text{-}Hz) - 10\log_{10}(B)
\end{equation}
The energy-per-bit to noise-density ratio $E_b/N_0$, which acts as the core digital indicator of link viability, is given by:
\begin{equation}
    E_b/N_0(dB) = C/N_0(dB\text{-}Hz) - 10\log_{10}(R_b)
\end{equation}
where $R_b$ is the information transmission bit rate in bps. The link margin is the excess power above the demodulator's threshold:
\begin{equation}
    Margin(dB) = E_b/N_0(dB) - Required\ E_b/N_0(dB)
\end{equation}

\subsection{Uplink-Downlink Coupling in Bent-Pipe Transponders}
Because a transparent geostationary bent-pipe transponder acts merely as a frequency-translating amplifier, the noise added on the uplink is amplified and retransmitted on the downlink. The end-to-end combined carrier-to-noise ratio $(C/N_0)_{total}$ is therefore coupled, and is calculated via inverse-linear addition of the individual uplink and downlink ratios:
\begin{equation}
    \left( \frac{C}{N_0} \right)_{total}^{-1} = \left( \frac{C}{N_0} \right)_{uplink}^{-1} + \left( \frac{C}{N_0} \right)_{downlink}^{-1}
\end{equation}
This indicates that the combined link margin will always be lower than the individual margins of either the uplink or the downlink alone, presenting a critical design trade-off in space communications.
