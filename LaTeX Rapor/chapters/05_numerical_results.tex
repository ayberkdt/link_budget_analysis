\begin{equation}
    \left( \frac{C}{N_0} \right)_{\text{total,linear}} = \left( 10^{-83.08 / 10} + 10^{-83.25 / 10} \right)^{-1}
\end{equation}
\begin{equation}
    \left( \frac{C}{N_0} \right)_{\text{total,dBHz}} = 10\log_{10}\left( \left( \frac{C}{N_0} \right)_{\text{total,linear}} \right) = 80.15\ \text{dB-Hz}

yielding a combined $E_b/N_0$ of $\ebnoTotal$ dB and a combined open-air link margin of \textbf{\marginClear~dB}. This margin closes the link above the required digital threshold of $7.0$ dB under clear-sky conditions.

\subsubsection{Parametric Sensitivity and 2D Contour Map Analysis}
The custom Python analyzer generates five distinct contour maps to study static system sensitivities, parameter trade-offs, and design boundaries. Each figure is analyzed below using a structured evaluation.

\subsubsection{Downlink C/N0 vs. GS2 Dish Diameter and Frequency}

\begin{figure}[H]
    \centering
    \includegraphics[width=\textwidth]{01_downlink_cn0_dish_vs_frequency.png}
    \caption{Downlink C/N0 vs GS2 Dish Diameter and Frequency.}
    \label{fig:contour01}
\end{figure}

\begin{itemize}
    \item \textbf{System Setup / Objective:} Investigate how the receiving antenna aperture size ($0.5$~m to $3.0$~m) and the downlink carrier frequency ($10.7$~GHz to $12.75$~GHz) jointly dictate the signal power density ($C/N_0$) at the receiver demodulator.
    \item \textbf{Numerical Observations:} The $C/N_0$ value scales from a minimum of approximately $81.5$~dB-Hz in the bottom-left corner (small dish, low frequency) to a maximum exceeding $98.0$~dB-Hz in the top-right corner (large dish, high frequency).
    \item \textbf{Physical / Engineering Interpretation:} Boresight antenna gain scales with the square of the physical diameter ($G \propto D^2$) and the square of the frequency ($G \propto f^2$). Although the free-space path loss also increases with frequency ($FSPL \propto f^2$), the receive antenna gain rises faster, resulting in a net $C/N_0$ increase towards the higher frequencies.
    \item \textbf{Design Guidelines:} Ground station terminal designers utilize this map to size the minimum dish diameter required for specific satellite transponder bands, ensuring that the receiver G/T remains high enough to close the link without paying for oversized hardware.
\end{itemize}

\subsubsection{Downlink Margin vs. Satellite EIRP and GS2 Tsys}

\begin{figure}[H]
    \centering
    \includegraphics[width=\textwidth]{02_downlink_margin_eirp_vs_tsys.png}
    \caption{Downlink Margin vs Satellite EIRP and GS2 Tsys.}
    \label{fig:contour02}
\end{figure}

\begin{itemize}
    \item \textbf{System Setup / Objective:} Map the boundary of link closure ($0$~dB margin limit) by trading off satellite transmit power (downlink EIRP, $40$~dBW to $55$~dBW) against the receiver noise performance ($T_{\text{sys}}$, $50$~K to $300$~K).
    \item \textbf{Numerical Observations:} A bold red $0$~dB contour line splits the parameter space. Any combination to the right of this line represents successful link closure. For instance, at a high receiver noise temp ($T_{\text{sys}} = 300$~K), the link requires a minimum satellite EIRP of $45.5$~dBW to close.
    \item \textbf{Physical / Engineering Interpretation:} The downlink margin varies linearly with satellite power in dB, but scales inversely with the logarithm of system noise temperature ($G/T \propto -10\log_{10} T_{\text{sys}}$). Higher thermal noise in the receiver LNB directly degrades the signal-to-noise ratio, requiring a compensating increase in transponder power.
    \item \textbf{Design Guidelines:} This trade-off is critical for cost optimization. Satellite operators can decide whether to spend capital on highly powerful satellites (high EIRP) or to invest in high-performance ground terminals (low $T_{\text{sys}}$ LNBs) to maintain a target link margin.
\end{itemize}

\subsubsection{Uplink C/N vs. HPA Power and GS1 Dish Diameter}

\begin{figure}[H]
    \centering
    \includegraphics[width=\textwidth]{03_uplink_cn_power_vs_dish.png}
    \caption{Uplink C/N vs HPA Power and GS1 Dish Diameter.}
    \label{fig:contour03}
\end{figure}

\begin{itemize}
    \item \textbf{System Setup / Objective:} Evaluate the trade-off between the uplink ground station High-Power Amplifier (HPA) wattage ($5$~W to $100$~W) and the uplink transmit antenna diameter ($1.2$~m to $4.5$~m) to achieve a target transponder Carrier-to-Noise ratio.
    \item \textbf{Numerical Observations:} To achieve a target uplink $C/N$ of $12$~dB, a small $1.2$~m dish requires the HPA to transmit at a high power of approximately $60$~Watts. Conversely, a larger $2.4$~m dish reduces the required HPA power to only $15$~Watts.
    \item \textbf{Physical / Engineering Interpretation:} Transmit gain increases by $6$~dB for every doubling of the antenna diameter. This focusing gain multiplies the raw HPA wattage, allowing a major reduction in actual transmitter power to achieve the same equivalent radiated power (EIRP).
    \item \textbf{Design Guidelines:} HPA hardware costs scale exponentially with output power. Ground terminal designers use this trade-off to minimize capital costs by choosing larger, less expensive dish antennas, which also has the critical benefit of narrowing the beamwidth and reducing side-lobe interference to adjacent satellites.
\end{itemize}

\subsubsection{Combined Eb/N0 vs. Information Bit Rate and Bandwidth}

\begin{figure}[H]
    \centering
    \includegraphics[width=\textwidth]{04_total_ebn0_bitrate_vs_bandwidth.png}
    \caption{Combined Eb/N0 vs Information Bit Rate and Bandwidth.}
    \label{fig:contour04}
\end{figure}

\begin{itemize}
    \item \textbf{System Setup / Objective:} Analyze how the combined end-to-end signal energy-per-bit ($E_b/N_0$) is affected by changes in the transponder's allocated bandwidth ($10$~MHz to $50$~MHz) and the information data transmission rate ($2$~Mbps to $50$~Mbps).
    \item \textbf{Numerical Observations:} The $E_b/N_0$ value drops sharply from a maximum exceeding $25$~dB at low bit rates ($2$~Mbps) down to less than $6$~dB at high transmission rates ($50$~Mbps), illustrating high sensitivity to data rate expansions.
    \item \textbf{Physical / Engineering Interpretation:} This represents the classic power-vs-bandwidth constraint of communications. Since $E_b/N_0 = C/N_0 - 10\log_{10}(R_b)$, sharing a finite carrier power $C$ over a higher number of bits per second ($R_b$) directly reduces the energy allocated to each individual bit.
    \item \textbf{Design Guidelines:} System engineers use this chart to select the maximum data rate that can be supported by the allocated transponder bandwidth without dropping the $E_b/N_0$ below the receiver's required Forward Error Correction (FEC) threshold, ensuring error-free baseband recovery.
\end{itemize}

\begin{itemize}

    \item \textbf{Numerical Observations:} Concentric contour rings illustrate the elevation angle dropping rapidly as latitude and longitude separations increase, falling below the standard $10^\circ$ and $5^\circ$ elevation engineering limits near the edges of the map.

    \item \textbf{Design Guidelines:} This map defines the geographic service coverage boundaries. For terminals operating in high-latitude zones (e.g., Northern Europe), system designers must allocate extra margins in the link budget to account for the severe path lengths and high tropospheric scintillation that occur at low elevation angles.
\end{itemize}

\subsection{Advanced Impairment and Dynamic Analysis}
This section introduces real-world atmospheric, orbital, and non-linear impairments to evaluate the system under worst-case operational conditions.
\subsubsection[Interference (ASI and IMD)]{Interference (ASI and IMD)\textsuperscript{*}}
\begin{itemize}
    \item \textbf{System Setup / Objective:} Evaluate the combined link degradation when Adjacent-Satellite Interference (ASI) and Traveling-Wave Tube Amplifier (TWTA) Intermodulation Distortion (IMD) are enabled. Adjacent satellites are modeled at $11.0^\circ$E and $15.0^\circ$E longitudes relative to our target satellite at $13.0^\circ$E.
    \item \textbf{Numerical Observations:} Enabling ASI and IMD drives the combined Carrier-to-Noise-and-Interference ratio down, reducing the total operating margin from the clear-sky baseline of $3.15$~dB down to \textbf{\marginASI~dB}.
    \item \textbf{Physical / Engineering Interpretation:} Co-channel power leakage from the adjacent satellites and non-linear intermodulation products generated within the satellite's multicarrier transponder act as extra noise sources, imposing a strict $0.37$~dB power penalty on the link.
    \item \textbf{Design Guidelines:} This result demonstrates that satellite operators cannot design links in isolation. Proper antenna side-lobe roll-off (to suppress ASI) and sufficient TWTA Output Back-Off (OBO $\approx 2.5$~dB to suppress IMD) must be strictly implemented to protect the link.
\end{itemize}

\subsubsection[Time-Varying Orbital Dynamics]{Time-Varying Orbital Dynamics\textsuperscript{*}}
\begin{itemize}
    \item \textbf{System Setup / Objective:} Simulate the impact of the satellite's geostationary three-axis station-keeping drift on the path loss and link margin over a continuous 48-hour window.
    \item \textbf{Numerical Observations:} The sub-satellite point drift introduces a $0.02$~dB peak-to-peak oscillation in the total link margin over a 48-hour period.
    \item \textbf{Physical / Engineering Interpretation:} Even nominally "geostationary" satellites drift within their assigned orbital station-keeping boxes due to lunar-solar gravitational perturbations and solar radiation pressure. This causes periodic variations in slant range (FSPL) and ground station elevation angles (atmospheric path length).
    \item \textbf{Design Guidelines:} While the $0.02$~dB variation is negligible for this robust link, it validates that the software is capable of accurately tracking dynamic physical effects, which becomes critical for LEO/MEO non-geostationary orbits.
\end{itemize}

\subsubsection[ITU-R Rain Fade and Atmospheric Impairments]{ITU-R Rain Fade and Atmospheric Impairments\textsuperscript{*}}
\begin{itemize}
    \item \textbf{System Setup / Objective:} Compute the excess atmospheric attenuation along the slant path under a $99.9\%$ link availability requirement (annual exceedance probability $p = 0.1\%$) using the ITU-R propagation standards.
    \item \textbf{Numerical Observations:}
    \begin{itemize}
        \item \textbf{Uplink Attenuation (14.0 GHz):} Total combined atmospheric fade of \upRainAtten~dB.
        \item \textbf{Downlink Attenuation (12.0 GHz):} Total combined atmospheric fade of \downRainAtten~dB.
    \end{itemize}
    \item \textbf{Physical / Engineering Interpretation:} Rain is the overwhelming cause of attenuation in the Ku-band, contributing to over $90\%$ of the total fade depth. Scintillation and cloud losses represent secondary effects, while gaseous absorption is highly static.
    \item \textbf{Design Guidelines:} Under these faded conditions, the combined link margin falls sharply to a negative \textbf{\marginRain~dB} (when interference is enabled). The link can no longer be closed with static thresholds. This indicates that Ku-band networks require extremely robust dynamic fade mitigation or larger hardware to survive heavy storms.
\end{itemize}

\subsubsection[DVB-S2 ACM Throughput Performance]{DVB-S2 ACM Throughput Performance\textsuperscript{*}}
\begin{itemize}
    \item \textbf{System Setup / Objective:} Evaluate how the DVB-S2 Adaptive Coding and Modulation (ACM) selector manages the transmission format during the $99.9\%$ availability rain fade event to prevent link outage.
    \item \textbf{Numerical Observations:} In clear-sky conditions, the link supports high-order spectral efficiencies. However, during the peak rain event, the ACM selector dynamically downshifts the MODCOD to highly robust \textbf{QPSK 2/5} (which requires a low threshold $E_s/N_0$ of only $-0.30$~dB), attempting to maintain link closure with a reduced net throughput of \textbf{\throughputRain~Mbps}.
    \item \textbf{Physical / Engineering Interpretation:} By dropping the modulation order from 32APSK/16APSK down to QPSK and increasing the FEC redundancy (rate 2/5), the receiver can decode signals that are heavily buried in atmospheric noise.
    \item \textbf{Design Guidelines:} This result demonstrates the practical benefit of ACM. Instead of suffering a complete link outage during the storm, the network dynamically trades off data rate (throughput drops) to attempt to maintain service availability, optimizing the transponder's annual capacity.
\end{itemize}

  \textbf{Interpretation of the Comparison:}
\begin{enumerate}
    \item \textbf{The Impact of Non-Linearities and Congestion:} Moving from a purely thermal-noise limited system to an interference-limited system (ASI + IMD) imposes an immediate $\approx 0.4$ dB power penalty. This demonstrates that simple static calculations dangerously overestimate link viability if adjacent orbital slots are congested or if the transponder is driven near saturation.
    \item \textbf{The Dominance of Atmospheric Fade:} The introduction of ITU-R rain fade models drastically alters the system state. The severe attenuation in the Ku-band consumes the available \marginASI~dB margin, plunging the system into a \marginRain~dB margin deficit at 99.9\% availability. This indicates that the current hardware sizing is technically insufficient for a strict 99.9\% SLA, requiring either more satellite EIRP, a larger GS2 dish, or relaxing the availability target.
    \item \textbf{Dynamic Compensation Strategy:} When the physical margin runs out during deep fade, the DVB-S2 ACM software stack takes over, aggressively downgrading the modulation scheme to highly robust QPSK 2/5 to decode buried signals. This sacrifices the data rate (down to \throughputRain~Mbps), attempting to maintain the link as long as physically possible before total outage.
\end{enumerate}

In synthesis, the staged analysis successfully demonstrates that the link is resilient. The static phase validates the hardware sizing, while the advanced phase confirms that the network can survive real-world operational stressors through a combination of raw RF power and adaptive digital signal processing.
