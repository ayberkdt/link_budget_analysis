\section[Atmospheric Propagation and ACM]{Atmospheric Propagation and ACM\footnote{All topics discussed in this chapter represent advanced, optional professional extensions developed beyond the core UZB451E term project specifications to model real-world satellite communication dynamics.}}

\vspace{0.3cm}

This chapter presents the standards-based atmospheric attenuation models and Adaptive Coding and Modulation (ACM) mechanisms implemented in this project. \textbf{These models are optional professional extensions representing an advanced study of link availability.}

\textit{Note: The implemented attenuation workflow strictly follows the cited ITU-R recommendation versions (e.g., P.618-13, P.837-7). Later ITU-R revisions may contain updated parameter databases or procedural refinements, but the baseline equations implemented in this analysis are kept consistent with the listed versions.}

\subsection[ITU-R Slant-Path Attenuation Suite]{ITU-R Slant-Path Attenuation Suite}

\subsubsection{The Engineering Problem}
Electromagnetic waves propagating along a slant path between a terrestrial ground station and a geostationary satellite traverse the Earth's troposphere, where they suffer from absorption and scattering. These impairments are caused by atmospheric gases (oxygen and water vapour), suspended water droplets in clouds, tropospheric turbulence, and liquid rain. At Ku-band frequencies, the physical wavelength of the signal is comparable to the size of falling raindrops, triggering severe Mie scattering and thermal absorption that can completely disrupt the communication link.

\subsubsection{Design Motivation and Rationale}
To design a reliable satellite communication system, engineers must accurately predict these atmospheric fades to calculate the required system margins. The International Telecommunication Union (ITU) publishes the ITU-R propagation recommendations---empirically validated mathematical suites that allow engineers to predict slant-path attenuation at any geographic coordinate on Earth. Analyzing these atmospheric effects is a mandatory step in high-frequency RF engineering.

\subsubsection{Mathematical Formulation}
The total atmospheric attenuation $A_{\text{total}}(p)$ in dB exceeded for an annual time percentage $p$ ($0.001\% \le p \le 5\%$) is calculated by combining gaseous absorption ($A_{\text{gaseous}}$), cloud attenuation ($A_{\text{cloud}}$), tropospheric scintillation ($A_{\text{scintillation}}$), and rain attenuation ($A_{\text{rain}}$) according to \textit{ITU-R P.618-13} \cite{itu618}:
\begin{equation}
    A_{\text{total}}(p) = A_{\text{gaseous}} + \sqrt{ \left( A_{\text{rain}}(p) + A_{\text{cloud}} \right)^2 + A_{\text{scintillation}}(p)^2 }
\end{equation}

For rain attenuation, the local point rainfall rate $R_{0.01}$, representing the rainfall intensity exceeded for $0.01\%$ of an average year, is obtained from \textit{ITU-R P.837} \cite{itu837}. The frequency- and polarization-dependent specific attenuation $\gamma_{\text{R}}$ in dB/km is then computed using power-law coefficients $k$ and $\alpha$ tabulated in \textit{ITU-R P.838-3} \cite{itu838}:
\begin{equation}
    \gamma_{\text{R}} = k R_{0.01}^\alpha
\end{equation}
The effective path length through the rain cell accounts for spatial localisation:
\begin{equation}
    L_{\text{eff}} = L_{\text{s}} \cdot r_{0.01} \cdot v_{0.01}
\end{equation}
where $r_{0.01}$ is the horizontal path reduction factor and $v_{0.01}$ is the vertical adjustment factor. The attenuation exceeded for $0.01\%$ of the year is $A_{0.01} = \gamma_{\text{R}} L_{\text{eff}}$. For other time percentages $p$:
\begin{equation}
    A_p = A_{0.01} \left( \frac{p}{0.01} \right)^{-\left[0.655 + 0.033\ln p - 0.045\ln A_{0.01} - \beta(1-p)\sin\theta\right]}
\end{equation}

Gaseous attenuation uses dry air and water vapor specific zenith attenuations scaled by the slant-path elevation angle (\textit{ITU-R P.676-12} \cite{itu676}):
\begin{equation}
    A_{\text{gaseous}} = \frac{\gamma_{\text{o}} h_{\text{o}} + \gamma_{\text{w}} h_{\text{w}}}{\sin\theta}
\end{equation}

Cloud liquid water attenuation uses columnar liquid water $M$ and specific attenuation $K_{\text{l}}$ (\textit{ITU-R P.840-8} \cite{itu840}):
\begin{equation}
    A_{\text{cloud}} = \frac{M \cdot K_{\text{l}}}{\sin\theta}
\end{equation}

Scintillation fade depth is scaled by the antenna aperture-averaging factor (\textit{ITU-R P.618-13} \cite{itu618}):
\begin{equation}
    A_{\text{scintillation}}(p) = a(p) \sigma
\end{equation}

\subsubsection{Operational Impact and Results}
This ITU-R suite provides the estimated fade depths that the RF link must tolerate. For our Rome-to-Ankara Ku-band link, at the 99.9\% availability design point, the model predicts \upRainAtten~dB uplink attenuation at 14 GHz and \downRainAtten~dB downlink attenuation at 12 GHz. When these fades are combined with interference and dynamic receiver noise, the end-to-end margin falls to \marginRain~dB.

\subsection[Monte-Carlo Rain Outage Simulation]{Monte-Carlo Rain Outage Simulation}

\subsubsection{The Engineering Problem}
The ITU-R recommendations provide static, deterministic bounds for link availability at specific annual time percentages. However, they do not simulate the chronological, time-varying behaviour of weather---the transitions between clear-sky and storm states---nor do they provide a probability distribution of the active link margin over a continuous meteorological time series.

\subsubsection{Design Motivation and Rationale}
Running a Monte Carlo simulation allows system engineers to generate thousands of random meteorological states based on an illustrative weather-state probability model. This educational stochastic simulation models the statistical variance of atmospheric fades, enabling computation of the estimated probability distribution of active link margins and the estimated annual availability.

\subsubsection{Mathematical Formulation}
The simulation engine executes $N = 8760$ discrete trials, representing each hour of a complete year. At each time step $t_i$, the atmospheric weather state $S(t_i)$ is sampled from a probability mass function:
\begin{itemize}
    \item \textbf{Clear-Sky ($S_0$):} Standard operating conditions ($0$~dB excess attenuation).
    \item \textbf{Light and Moderate Rain ($S_1, S_2$):} Small to medium fade depths drawn from a uniform attenuation profile.
    \item \textbf{Heavy Storms ($S_3$):} Rare events ($p < 0.5\%$) triggering severe attenuation (up to $15$~dB in Ku-band).
\end{itemize}
For each generated hourly weather state, the computed active Carrier-to-Noise ratio and link margin are recalculated:
\begin{equation}
    Margin(t_i) = E_b/N_0(t_i) - \text{Required } E_b/N_0
\end{equation}
An \textit{outage event} is any hour where $Margin(t_i) < 0$. The overall system availability is:
\begin{equation}
    \text{Availability (\%)} = 100 \times \left( 1 - \frac{\sum \mathbf{1}(Margin(t_i) < 0)}{N} \right)
\end{equation}

\subsubsection{Operational Impact and Results}
The Monte Carlo simulation outputs a probability distribution of the link margin, illustrating the estimated risk profile over the simulated year. This allows the designer to evaluate whether the selected static margin is sufficient under stochastic weather variations.

\textbf{Note:} This Monte Carlo model is an illustrative stochastic availability simulation based on assumed weather-state probabilities (light rain: 2.0\%, moderate rain: 0.6\%, heavy rain: 0.1\%), not a site-calibrated meteorological time-series model derived from measured annual precipitation data. Results should therefore be interpreted as \textit{estimated availability under the assumed stochastic weather-state model}, not as a prediction of actual annual availability.

\subsection[DVB-S2 ACM (Adaptive Coding \& Modulation)]{DVB-S2 ACM (Adaptive Coding \& Modulation)}

\subsubsection{The Engineering Problem}
Traditionally, satellite links are designed for the worst-case rain fade scenario using a fixed MODCOD. Because severe storms occur for less than $1\%$ of the year, this approach means that for over $99\%$ of the year the link operates with a large excess margin, wasting potential channel capacity.

\subsubsection{Design Motivation and Rationale}
Designing with a fixed MODCOD forces a severe compromise: either accept high outages during rain or accept extremely low data rates. Adaptive Coding and Modulation (ACM) solves this by dynamically monitoring the active $E_s/N_0$ at the receiver and changing the modulation order and FEC code rate in real-time. This maximises throughput during clear-sky conditions while mitigating outage risk during heavy storms by switching to highly robust MODCODs.

\subsubsection{Mathematical Formulation}
The receiver continuously monitors $(E_s/N_0)_{\text{available}}$ and dynamically selects the highest spectral efficiency $\eta$ (in bits/symbol) from the DVB-S2 \cite{dvbs2} MODCOD ladder that satisfies:
\begin{equation}
    (E_s/N_0)_{\text{available}} \ge \text{Required } (E_s/N_0)_{\text{MODCOD}} + Margin_{\text{impl}}
\end{equation}

\begin{figure}[H]
    \centering
    \includegraphics[width=0.75\textwidth]{28_dvbs2_modcod_ladder.png}
    \caption{DVB-S2 MODCOD ladder: trade-off between spectral efficiency and required signal-to-noise ratio.}
    \label{fig:dvbs2_modcod_ladder}
\end{figure}

Using the usable symbol rate $R_{\text{s}} = B / (1 + \alpha_{\text{rolloff}})$, the active net throughput is:
\begin{equation}
    Throughput(\text{Mbps}) = \eta \cdot R_{\text{s}}
\end{equation}

\subsubsection{Operational Impact and Results}
Under clear-sky conditions, the ACM selector shifts to a higher-efficiency MODCOD than the robust rain-fade mode. The exact selected mode depends on the available $E_s/N_0$ and the implemented DVB-S2 threshold table. For a 36 MHz carrier with $\alpha=0.20$ ($R_s = 30$ Msps), the available $E_s/N_0$ allows for QPSK 3/4 in purely thermal-noise conditions, and degrades to QPSK 2/5 under heavy rain.

\subsection[Uplink Power Control (ULPC)]{Uplink Power Control (ULPC)}

ULPC is discussed as a mitigation concept, not as a fully implemented closed-loop control model. When heavy rain attenuates the uplink carrier, the satellite transponder input power drops below its optimal operating region. ULPC addresses this by dynamically increasing the ground station HPA transmit power:
\begin{equation}
    P_{\text{tx,dynamic}}(\text{dBW}) = P_{\text{tx,nominal}}(\text{dBW}) + A_{\text{rain,up}}(\text{dB})
\end{equation}
subject to $P_{\text{tx,dynamic}} \le P_{\text{tx,max}}$. This expression represents ideal first-order fade compensation. In practice, ULPC is constrained by HPA headroom, regulatory EIRP limits, adjacent-satellite interference budgets, transponder input back-off requirements, and the finite loop latency of the measurement-and-correction cycle. Therefore, the full recovery of the uplink attenuation through power control alone cannot always be guaranteed, and ULPC is best treated as a complementary measure alongside DVB-S2 ACM.
