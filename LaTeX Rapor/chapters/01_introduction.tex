\section{Introduction}

For decades, humanity has gazed at the stars, but in the mid-20th century, we began looking back down at Earth to connect our global civilization. The concept of placing a wireless repeater in a geostationary equatorial orbit—famously envisioned by science-fiction writer and engineer Arthur C. Clarke in 1945—has transformed from a bold dream into the invisible backbone of modern telecommunications. Today, satellite links bridge continents, broadcast live events to millions, synchronize global financial markets, and provide crucial communications in the aftermath of terrestrial network failures. This term project presents an in-depth, hands-on academic study of these remarkable space-based networks, mapping out the full physical layer of a modern satellite link.

\subsection{Fundamental Principles and Orbital Mechanics}
At the heart of geostationary satellite communications lies a beautiful dance of celestial mechanics and electromagnetic physics. Dictated by Kepler's Third Law of Planetary Motion, placing a satellite in a circular orbit directly above the Earth's equator at an altitude of approximately 35,786~km (resulting in a semi-major axis of 42,164~km) ensures the satellite's orbital period matches the Earth's sidereal rotation exactly (approximately 23 hours, 56 minutes, and 4 seconds). 

To an observer on the ground, the satellite appears completely frozen at a fixed longitude in the sky. This simple orbital trick yields massive engineering benefits: ground stations do not need complex, continuous mechanical tracking systems. Instead, a simple parabolic dish antenna can be pointed at a single coordinate in the sky and remain locked onto the satellite indefinitely. However, achieving this "fixed" link requires careful RF link budget calculations to overcome the immense free-space path loss (FSPL)—often exceeding 200~dB—accumulated over a 37,000~km journey through the vacuum of space and the Earth's atmospheric layers. Furthermore, slight gravitational perturbations from the Sun and Moon require the satellite to perform periodic station-keeping maneuvers, causing tiny apparent orbital drifts that must be accounted for in the system margins.

\subsection{Bent-Pipe Transponder Architecture}
The satellite payload modeled in this project is a transparent, classic \textit{bent-pipe} transponder. Unlike modern regenerative payloads (which feature On-Board Processing to demodulate, error-correct, and remodulate the signal in space), a bent-pipe satellite acts as a simple, analog "mirror in the sky." 

The payload is transparent: it captures the weak uplink signal arriving from Earth, filters out out-of-band noise using a Low-Noise Amplifier (LNA), translates the carrier frequency to the downlink band using a local oscillator and mixer, amplifies the signal to high power using a Traveling-Wave Tube Amplifier (TWTA) or a Solid-State Power Amplifier (SSPA), and retransmits it back to the ground. 

While this simplicity makes the satellite incredibly robust, cost-effective, and format-agnostic (capable of carrying analog TV, digital DVB-S2, or IP data indiscriminately), it introduces a major engineering challenge: uplink noise and adjacent-channel interference are amplified and retransmitted right along with the desired signal. As a result, the uplink and downlink link budgets are physically coupled. The system engineer must ensure the combined, end-to-end carrier-to-noise density ratio ($(C/N_0)_{total}$) is strong enough for reliable demodulation at the receiving ground station.

\subsection{Frequency Allocation and Atmospheric Physics}
This project explores a high-capacity link operating in the Ku-band (12--18~GHz), which is the commercial workhorse for Direct-to-Home (DTH) television broadcasting and VSAT business networks. Our uplink operates at a nominal frequency of 14.0~GHz, while the downlink is frequency-translated to 12.0~GHz. We utilize circular polarization (with a polarization tilt angle $\tau = 45^\circ$), which eliminates the need for precise mechanical polarization alignment at the earth terminals and provides excellent immunity to Faraday rotation effects in the ionosphere.

The Ku-band frequency regime represents a sweet spot in space communications. It provides narrow, highly directional beam patterns and high antenna gains for compact, inexpensive dish sizes, preventing interference with adjacent satellites. However, this high frequency has a major vulnerability: atmospheric hydrometeors (water molecules). Because the physical wavelength of Ku-band signals (approx. 2--2.5~cm) is comparable to the size of large raindrops, the signals suffer from severe Mie scattering and absorption. Rain, dense clouds, and atmospheric gases attenuate Ku-band signals, presenting a highly dynamic propagation challenge. To prevent complete system outages during storms, this attenuation must be rigorously modeled using stochastic weather data and mitigated using adaptive techniques.

\subsection{Scenario Definition}
To ground our study in a realistic commercial context, we model a high-capacity, one-way transparent link between two historic capitals—Rome and Ankara—connecting Europe and Anatolia via the commercial \textbf{Eutelsat Hotbird 13G} satellite:
\begin{equation*}
    \text{GS1 (Rome)} \xrightarrow{\text{Uplink: } 14.0\text{ GHz}} \text{Eutelsat Hotbird 13G (13E)} \xrightarrow{\text{Downlink: } 12.0\text{ GHz}} \text{GS2 (Ankara)}
\end{equation*}
\begin{itemize}
    \item \textbf{Ground Station 1 (GS1 Rome):} Located in Rome, Italy ($41.9028^\circ$ N, $12.4964^\circ$ E). It acts as the broadcasting terminal (uplink station), utilizing a 2.4-meter parabolic dish antenna with a 20.0~Watt high-power amplifier (HPA).
    \item \textbf{GEO Satellite (Hotbird 13G):} Positioned at $13.0^\circ$ E longitude on the geostationary equatorial arc. It is modeled with a constant downlink EIRP footprint value of 50.0~dBW over the coverage zone and a receive G/T value of 3.0~dB/K at the uplink beam.
    \item \textbf{Ground Station 2 (GS2 Ankara):} Located in Ankara, Turkey ($39.9334^\circ$ N, $32.8597^\circ$ E). It acts as the receive-only downlink terminal, utilizing a 0.9-meter parabolic dish receiver with a typical consumer-grade noise temperature ($T_{sys}$) of 150.0~Kelvin.
\end{itemize}

\subsection{The Custom Python Software Suite}
A major distinguishing feature of this project is the development of a fully custom, modular Python software suite to perform the link budget analysis. Rather than relying on static Excel spreadsheets, the project includes an object-oriented calculation engine capable of rendering 3D contour plots, running Monte-Carlo rain simulations, and calculating exact orbital geometries. The tool operates as a powerful command-line interface (CLI) workflow, allowing users to dynamically modify network topologies, test system sensitivities, and compute advanced atmospheric degradation models in an automated, reproducible manner.

\subsection{Structure of the Report}
This report provides a structured, comprehensive analysis of this geostationary link. Chapter 2 details the classical link budget equations based on Pratt's textbook to establish our open-sky baseline. Chapter 3 presents advanced optional modeling layers (such as adjacent satellite interference, intermodulation distortion, digital BER/FEC, dynamic system noise temperature, and apparent GEO orbital drift). Chapter 4 models the standards-based ITU-R P.618 atmospheric suite alongside a DVB-S2 Adaptive Coding and Modulation (ACM) selector. Chapter 5 discusses the numerical results and provides detailed physical interpretations of our generated 2D contour maps, followed by a concluding summary in Chapter 6.
