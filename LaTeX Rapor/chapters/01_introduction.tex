\section{Introduction}

\subsection{Fundamental Principles of Satellite Communications}
Satellite communication represents a cornerstone of modern global telecommunication infrastructure, enabling high-capacity voice, video, and data transmission over vast geographical distances. Unlike terrestrial links, which are limited by line-of-sight constraints, geographical obstacles, and regional infrastructure deficits, space-based communication networks leverage orbiting platforms to establish wide-area broadcast and point-to-point connections. 

A geostationary earth orbit (GEO) satellite is positioned in a circular orbit directly above the Earth's equator at a nominal altitude of approximately \SI{35786}{\km} (corresponding to a semi-major axis or orbit radius of \SI{42164}{\km}). At this specific distance, the orbital period of the satellite matches the Earth's sidereal rotation period ($T_{sidereal} \approx \SI{23.934}{\hour}$). Consequently, as viewed from any ground station on the Earth's surface, the satellite appears stationary at a fixed longitudinal position in the sky. This characteristic is highly advantageous as it allows ground station antennas to remain pointed at the satellite without requiring complex, continuous tracking systems, thereby reducing mechanical and control complexity at the earth terminals.

\subsection{Bent-Pipe Transponder Architecture}
The system modeled in this term project is a traditional one-way \textit{bent-pipe} (transparent) geostationary transponder. In a bent-pipe architecture, the satellite does not demodulate or process the payload data. Instead, it acts as a space-based repeater. The satellite's payload receive antenna intercepts the weak uplink carrier, which is then routed through a low-noise amplifier (LNA) to minimize noise figure contribution. The received signal is frequency-translated from the uplink band to the downlink band using a local oscillator and mixer, amplified via a traveling-wave tube amplifier (TWTA) or a solid-state power amplifier (SSPA) to the desired output power, and retransmitted through the downlink transmit antenna to the earth terminal. 

This transparent repeat-only mechanism makes the satellite payload independent of the transmission modulation, framing, or coding protocols, providing extreme operational flexibility. However, it also implies that any uplink noise, interference, and distortions are amplified and retransmitted directly to the downlink, coupling the uplink and downlink link budgets.

\subsection{Frequency Allocation and Polarization}
The communications link operates in the Ku-band, which is widely utilized for satellite broadcasting (Direct-To-Home, DTH) and Very Small Aperture Terminal (VSAT) data networks. The uplink operates at a nominal carrier frequency of \SI{14.0}{\GHz}, while the downlink is translated to \SI{12.0}{\GHz}. Circular polarization is selected (polarization tilt angle $\tau = 45^\circ$) to eliminate the necessity for precise mechanical polarization alignment at the earth terminals, providing robustness against ionospheric Faraday rotation.

The Ku-band frequency band offers an optimal trade-off in satellite system design. It provides highly directional beam patterns and high antenna gains for moderate dish sizes, preventing excessive co-channel interference with adjacent geostationary satellites. However, Ku-band signals are susceptible to atmospheric water molecules, meaning that rain fade, gaseous absorption, and cloud attenuation present significant propagation challenges that must be accounted for in the overall system design.

\subsection{Scenario Definition}
The specific scenario modeled in this study consists of a one-way geostationary communication link through an commercial GEO satellite placed nominally at $42^\circ$ East longitude:
\begin{equation*}
    \text{GS1 (Istanbul)} \xrightarrow{\text{Uplink: } \SI{14.0}{\GHz}} \text{GEO Satellite (42E)} \xrightarrow{\text{Downlink: } \SI{12.0}{\GHz}} \text{GS2 (Ankara)}
\end{equation*}
*   \textbf{Ground Station 1 (GS1 Istanbul):} Located in Istanbul, Turkey ($41.0082^\circ$ N, $28.9784^\circ$ E, altitude \SI{0.0}{\km}). It acts as the broadcasting terminal (transmitting earth station), utilizing a \SI{2.4}{\m} parabolic dish antenna with a \SI{20.0}{\watt} high-power amplifier (HPA).
*   \textbf{GEO Satellite (42E):} Positioned at $42.0^\circ$ E longitude on the geostationary equatorial arc. It is modeled with a constant downlink EIRP footprint value of \SI{50.0}{\dBW} over the coverage zone and a receive G/T value of \SI{3.0}{\dB/\kelvin} at the uplink beam.
*   \textbf{Ground Station 2 (GS2 Ankara):} Located in Ankara, Turkey ($39.9334^\circ$ N, $32.8597^\circ$ E, altitude \SI{0.0}{\km}). It acts as the receive-only terminal, utilizing a \SI{0.9}{\m} parabolic dish receiver with a receiver system noise temperature ($T_{sys}$) of \SI{150.0}{\kelvin}.
