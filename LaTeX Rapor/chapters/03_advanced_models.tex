\subsection[Adjacent-Satellite Interference (ASI) and IMD]{Adjacent-Satellite Interference (ASI) and IMD\textsuperscript{*}}

\subsubsection{The Engineering Problem}
In a crowded geostationary orbit (GEO) arc, satellites are spaced closely together, typically separated by only $2.0^\circ$ to $3.0^\circ$ of longitude. Consequently, ground station antenna beams, which have finite beamwidths, inevitably illuminate adjacent satellites, causing co-channel and cross-polar power leakage. This is known as Adjacent-Satellite Interference (ASI). Concurrently, inside the satellite transponder, multiple carrier signals are amplified by a single Traveling-Wave Tube Amplifier (TWTA). When operated near its saturation region, the amplifier's non-linear behavior causes the carriers to mix, generating unwanted Intermodulation Distortion (IMD) products that fall directly within the desired channel bandwidth.

\subsubsection{Design Motivation and Rationale}
Analyzing ASI and IMD is critical because these interference sources degrade the receiver's operating environment from a standard thermal noise channel to an interference-limited channel. Neglecting these impairments leads to overly optimistic link margins, risking severe service degradation or complete link outage in real-world multi-carrier environments. Modeling these factors allows system engineers to compute the true, end-to-end signal quality and determine the exact power levels needed to close the link safely.

\subsubsection{Mathematical Formulation}
The off-axis discrimination angle $\theta_{\text{off}}$ between the target satellite and the adjacent interferer is calculated using their respective ECEF position vectors. The off-axis gain $G_{\text{off}}(\theta_{\text{off}})$ in the direction of the interferer is estimated using the standard ITU-R off-axis antenna envelope approximation:
\begin{equation}
    G_{\text{off}}(\theta_{\text{off}}) = \min\left( G_{\text{max}} - 3.0,\ 32.0 - 25\log_{10}(\theta_{\text{off}}) \right)
\end{equation}
The individual carrier-to-interference ($C/I$) ratios for uplink, downlink, and intermodulation are combined with the thermal carrier-to-noise ($C/N$) ratios using the inverse-linear harmonic summation:
\begin{equation}
    \left( \frac{C}{N+I} \right)_{\text{total}}^{-1} = \left( \frac{C}{N} \right)_{\text{uplink}}^{-1} + \left( \frac{C}{N} \right)_{\text{downlink}}^{-1} + \left( \frac{C}{I_{\text{ASI,up}}} \right)^{-1} + \left( \frac{C}{I_{\text{ASI,down}}} \right)^{-1} + \left( \frac{C}{I_{\text{IMD}}} \right)^{-1}
\end{equation}

\subsubsection{Operational Impact and Results}
The combined carrier-to-noise-and-interference ratio $C/(N+I)_{\text{total}}$ acts as the ultimate performance bottleneck, representing the true signal quality at the receiver. In high-density satellite arcs, ASI and IMD can reduce the overall link margin by $1.5$ to $3.0$~dB. To mitigate these effects, operators must increase ground dish diameters (narrowing the beamwidth $\theta_{\text{HPBW}}$) or operate the satellite TWTA with sufficient back-off to suppress IMD generation.

\subsection[Hardware Impairments]{Hardware Impairments: Non-Linearity and Phase Noise\textsuperscript{*}}

In practice, the satellite transponder is not an ideal linear amplifier. The TWTA exhibits AM/AM compression and AM/PM conversion near saturation, while local oscillator phase noise rotates the received constellation points. These effects are collectively captured by two simplified parameters in our link budget:
\begin{itemize}
    \item \textbf{Output Back-Off (OBO):} A deliberate reduction of the operating point below saturation to suppress non-linear distortion:
    \begin{equation}
        EIRP_{\text{active}}(\text{dBW}) = EIRP_{\text{sat}}(\text{dBW}) - OBO(\text{dB})
    \end{equation}
    \item \textbf{Implementation Loss ($L_{\text{impl}}$):} A composite penalty (typically 1.0--2.0~dB) accounting for residual phase noise, modem non-idealities, and filter imperfections:
    \begin{equation}
        \left( \frac{E_b}{N_0} \right)_{\text{effective}} = \left( \frac{E_b}{N_0} \right)_{\text{nominal}} - L_{\text{impl}}
    \end{equation}
\end{itemize}
For the present study, an OBO of 2.5~dB and a total implementation loss of 1.2~dB are assumed, consistent with typical Ku-band TWTA operating practices \cite{pratt}.

\subsection[Digital Baseband Performance Metrics]{Digital Baseband Performance Metrics\textsuperscript{*}}

The satellite link must meet a strict Quality of Service, typically $\text{BER} \le 10^{-7}$ for IP data. For coherent QPSK over AWGN, the uncoded bit error rate is:
\begin{equation}
    P_{b,\text{uncoded}} = \frac{1}{2} \text{erfc}\left( \sqrt{\frac{E_b}{N_0}} \right)
\end{equation}
With FEC coding (e.g., DVB-S2 LDPC+BCH), a coding gain $G_{\text{code}} \approx 6$--$8$~dB shifts the operating point, enabling reliable reception at much lower raw $E_b/N_0$ values and pushing the link efficiency close to the Shannon limit:
\begin{equation}
    C(\text{bps}) = B \log_{2}\left( 1 + SNR \right)
\end{equation}
This coding framework is the foundation for the DVB-S2 MODCOD ladder used by the ACM selector in Chapter~4.

\subsection[Dynamic System Noise Temperature]{Dynamic System Noise Temperature\textsuperscript{*}}

\subsubsection{The Engineering Problem}
In basic clear-sky link calculations, the ground station receiver system noise temperature $T_{\text{sys}}$ is assumed to be a static constant. However, in physical links, $T_{\text{sys}}$ fluctuates dynamically. When the ground antenna points at a low elevation angle $\theta$, the signal must traverse a longer atmospheric path, which increases the absorbed and re-emitted background sky noise. More critically, during rain events, the lossy water molecules in the rain path absorb RF energy and simultaneously emit thermal noise, heating up the receiver's environment.

\subsubsection{Design Motivation and Rationale}
Neglecting the dynamic nature of $T_{\text{sys}}$ during atmospheric fading events leads to a major ``double-penalty'' error in system design. Rain does not only attenuate the incoming carrier power ($C$ drops), but it also simultaneously increases the system thermal noise power ($N_0$ spikes). Modeling this dynamic thermal emission is absolutely essential to compute the exact, coupled Carrier-to-Noise ratio degradation under dynamic weather conditions.

\subsubsection{Mathematical Formulation}
The dynamic, elevation- and fade-dependent system noise temperature $T_{\text{sys}}$ is modeled at the LNA input as:
\begin{equation}
    T_{\text{sys}}(\theta, A_{\text{rain}}) = T_{\text{receiver}} + T_{\text{spillover}} + T_{\text{sky}}(\theta) + T_{\text{rain}}(A_{\text{rain}})
\end{equation}
where $T_{\text{sky}}(\theta)$ is the clear-sky noise at elevation angle $\theta$, and $T_{\text{rain}}$ is the rain thermal emission noise calculated using the radiative transfer equation:
\begin{equation}
    T_{\text{rain}} = T_{\text{atm}} \left( 1 - 10^{-A_{\text{rain}}/10} \right)
\end{equation}
where $T_{\text{atm}} = 275$~Kelvin represents the physical atmospheric temperature of the lossy rain medium.

\subsubsection{Operational Impact and Results}
During a heavy Ku-band storm causing an attenuation of $A_{\text{rain}} = 10.0$~dB, the rain noise contribution $T_{\text{rain}}$ spikes by approximately $247.5$~Kelvin. For a high-performance ground receiver with a clear-sky $T_{\text{sys}} = 150.0$~K, this storm nearly triples the total system noise to $397.5$~K. This dynamic thermal noise increase adds a $4.2$~dB penalty directly to the link margin on top of the physical $10.0$~dB signal attenuation, demonstrating why dynamic modeling is vital.

\subsection[Apparent GEO Orbit Motion]{Apparent GEO Orbit Motion\textsuperscript{*}}

Although geostationary satellites are nominally ``fixed'', gravitational perturbations cause three-axis oscillations within the station-keeping box. The satellite's deviations are modeled as:
\begin{align}
    \Delta \lambda(t) &= A_{\text{ew}} \sin\left(\frac{2\pi t}{T_{\text{sidereal}}} + \phi_{\text{ew}}\right) \\
    \Delta \phi(t) &= A_{\text{ns}} \sin\left(\frac{2\pi t}{T_{\text{sidereal}}} + \phi_{\text{ns}}\right) \\
    \Delta r(t) &= A_{\text{r}} \sin\left(\frac{2\pi t}{T_{\text{sidereal}}/2} + \phi_{\text{r}}\right)
\end{align}
These positional drifts dynamically update the slant range vector $\vec{\rho}(t)$, producing a daily ``breathing'' of the link margin typically within $\pm 0.2$~dB. Apparent GEO motion is not the dominant limitation for the selected link, but it provides a useful pointing robustness check. For the large uplink dish ($\gsOneDish$~m, narrow $\theta_{\text{HPBW}}$), even a small drift can warrant step-tracking, whereas the consumer-grade $\gsTwoDish$~m receive dish remains stably locked without tracking.
